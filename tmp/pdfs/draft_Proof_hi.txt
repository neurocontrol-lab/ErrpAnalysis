ABSTRACT
Eye-hand coordination is the ability to perform precise adjustments of movements in 
response to unexpected visual changes. In the human brain, such ability is neurally encoded 
by a cortical network centered on the intraparietal sulcus (IPS), as shown by the impairment 
of visuo-motor skills following a decrease of cortical excitability of IPS. On this basis, we 
hypothesized that an excitatory neuromodulation of IPS (inverse of decrease) could result in 
enhanced visuo-motor skills (opposite of impairment). To test this hypothesis, we increased 
the cortical excitability of IPS in 28 healthy participants via high-definition transcranial direct 
current stimulation (HD-tDCS; anodal, 1.5mA, 20min), and we assessed its impact on visuo-
motor coordination. To this aim, we used a double-step paradigm task in which, once a 
reaching movement started, the target suddenly moved to a new location, thus requiring an 
online visuo-motor correction. We observed significant effects of the excitatory 
neuromodulation of IPS on the temporal aspects of the movement. The time to start the 
correction with respect to the total movement time was shorter, and the time to reach the 
peak velocity was longer. In contrast, the spatial dynamics of the movement were not 
affected. The dissociation between the affected temporal and unaffected spatial aspects of 
visuo-motor coordination suggests that IPS encodes the appropriate timing to accomplish 
visuo-motor adjustments, independently of the movement kinematics. These findings add 
evidence on facilitatory visuo-motor effects of excitatory neuromodulation of IPS, opening up 
novel perspectives for physical training and visuo-motor neurorehabilitation.

1. INTRODUCTION
The elegance and efficiency of human movements owes much to our ability to compensate 
for initial inaccuracies and environmental perturbations. The ability to perform online 
corrections relies on real-time monitoring of the hand/body trajectory from the onset of the 
movement (Bard et al. 1999; Prablanc et al. 1986; Goodale et al. 1986). Several studies have 
implicated the posterior parietal cortex in the brain network that encodes the visuo-motor 
coordination required to update a movement (Desmurget et al. 1999; Grea et al. 2002; 
Andersen et al. 1997; Pisella et al. 2000). Within this network, the role of the intraparietal 
sulcus (IPS) in monitoring visually-guided reaching movements has been widely supported by 
neurophysiological (Sakata et al. 1995), brain imaging (Clower et al. 1996; Culham et al. 2003; 
Frey et al. 2005), and clinical evidence (Khan et al. 2013). Additionally, the use of non-invasive 
brain stimulation techniques demonstrated the causative role of inhibitory neuromodulation 
of IPS on impairing the capacity to correct ongoing reaching movements (Desmurget et al. 
1999; Rodriguez-Herreros et al. 2015; Della-Maggiore et al. 2004; Panico et al. 2018). This 
evidence establishes the implication of IPS dysfunctioning in visuo-motor impairments. In the 
same vein but in opposite direction, we hypothesized that an excitatory (opposite of 
inhibitory) neuromodulation of IPS could result in the enhancement (opposite of impairment) 
of certain aspects of visuo-motor coordination. This might have important outcomes for 
performance training and neurorehabilitation. With this rationale and motivations, the 
present study assessed the facilitatory effects of excitatory neuromodulation of IPS on visuo-
motor coordination.
To this aim, we used transcranial direct current stimulation, a non-invasive brain 
stimulation technique that applies a weak electrical current to the scalp, which partially 
passes through the cortex, de- or hyper-polarizes neurons and thereby alters cortical 
excitability of a relatively large area (Nitsche and Paulus 2000; Polania et al. 2018; Romero 
Lauro et al. 2014). Experiments using transcranial direct current stimulation in traditional 
settings showed its ability to modulate visuo-motor skills (Panico et al. 2018). Furthermore, 
the use of a high-definition montage of the transcranial Direct Current Stimulation (hereafter 
"HD-tDCS"), improves the focality of the induced cortical electric field, thus allowing for more 
spatially precise stimulation protocols (Datta et al. 2009). The capacity of anodal HD-tDCS 
(excitatory) to modulate cortical excitability and the associated visuo-motor learning 
rendered it particularly attractive for interacting with motor enhancement both in

experimental (Vollmann et al. 2013) and rehabilitation settings, including stroke (Kang et al. 
2016; Reis et al. 2009), Parkinson’s disease (von Papen et al. 2014), and focal hand dystonia

Online eye-hand coordination has often been investigated via ‘double-step’ reaching 
tasks, in which the target of a reaching movement suddenly moves to a new location after 
the movement onset. In the present study, we investigated whether excitatory 
neuromodulation of IPS through anodal HD-tDCS affected the ability to adjust ongoing 
movements using such double-step paradigm. We quantified the effects of excitatory 
neuromodulation of IPS on the temporal and spatial aspects of the movement by comparing 
them at three different time points: baseline (hereafter "pre-HDtDCS"); after the excitatory 
neuromodulation of IPS (hereafter "POST-HDtDCS"), and in a re-test session in which 
participants performed the same task 24 hours later, once the HDtDCS effects were vanished 
(hereafter "Control").
2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. Participants
Twenty-eight healthy right-handed volunteers (11 women; mean age 24.9 ± 5.8 years) 
participated in the present study. All participants were naïve with respect to the experimental 
procedures and the hypothesis of the study. Participants had normal or corrected-to-normal 
visual acuity and reported no history of neuropsychiatric disorders. Prior to their inclusion in 
the study, participants signed a written informed consent. The study was performed 
according to the declaration of Helsinki 2013 and was approved by the local Ethics 
Committee. All participants were screened for HD-tDCS tolerance (Nitsche and Paulus 2000; 
Woods et al. 2016). The Edinburgh handedness inventory (Oldfield 1971) was administered 
to assess handedness.
2.2. Apparatus and data acquisition
Participants were seated in front of a table positioned 45-50cm below their eyes. Visual 
stimuli (see below) were generated using Python v3.6 running on Windows 10 in an Intel Core 
i7-7700 3.6Ghz computer and displayed on a 24” HP E232 monitor with a refresh rate of 60 
Hz and a resolution of 1920 x 1080 pixels. A Logitech G G203 Prodigy cable mouse was used

to track the hand’s 2D spatial position during reaching movements. The sampling rate of the 
mouse was set to 250 Hz. Time series of the recorded individual position coordinates were 
processed with a low-pass Butterworth filter (cut-off frequency of 6 Hz) for further analysis 
(Mason et al. 2001; Rodríguez-Herreros and López-Moliner 2011). Missing samples in 
recorded coordinates due to erratic orientations of the optical sensor were interpolated off-
line (Tunik et al. 2005) using spline functions (Liu and McMillan 2006). 
2.3. Visual stimuli and procedure
For each trial, the experimental task consisted of performing a fast goal-directed reaching 
movement towards a visual target located in the center of the screen (Figure 1A). Prior to the 
initiation of the trial, participants were required to move the mouse to the starting position 
in the bottom center of the screen. After 1000ms with the mouse placed at this starting point, 
a small white fixation cross was automatically shown as a warning signal in the center of the 
screen. The fixation cross preceded the target onset for a variable period (300 or 800ms) to 
avoid participants from predicting the timing of that onset. Participants were asked to fixate 
the point until a target appeared in the center of the screen. Trials with (34%) and without 
(66%) target displacement were presented in pseudorandom order. In undisplaced trials, the 
target remained static in the center of the screen. In displaced trials, however, the target 
showed an unexpected 10.5cm (400 pixels) lateral displacement –to the left or to the right 
with a 50-50% chance–, after the initiation of the reaching movement. The displacement was 
timed at the movement onset to assure that participants did not have relevant visual cues 
about the final position of the target during the initial movement planning. To this aim, the 
movement onset was detected by a specific velocity threshold of the mouse movement (see 
Analysis section). The total longitudinal distance between the starting point and the target 
was 30cm (1200 pixels) in the screen. The ratio between the distance travelled by the mouse 
in the real world and the cursor in the screen was 2:3, that is, when the mouse moved by 
20cm, the cursor advanced 30cm in the screen. To discard trials with long reaction times, a 
warning sound was provided when the velocity threshold required to start the movement 
was not reached during the 500ms that followed the target onset. Participants were 
instructed to hit the target as fast and as accurate as possible performing a ballistic movement

When the target was displaced, participants had to adjust their hand trajectory to succeed in 
hitting the target in its final location. The target was presented for 1000ms. At the end of the

to start the next trial. Participants were instructed not to move their trunk with respect to the 
chair. Head movements were allowed to avoid that participant’s behavior could be different 
than in natural conditions(Steinman et al. 1990).
2.4. Experimental design
An overview of the experimental setup is shown in Figure 1B. The study comprised three 
different conditions: pre-HDtDCS, POST-HDtDCS, and Control. The first session (pre-HDtDCS 
condition) was a baseline measurement performed prior to the HD-tDCS protocol. In the 
second session (POST-HDtDCS condition) participants repeated the task immediately after the 
HD-tDCS protocol. The third and final session (Control condition) was a re-test performed the 
day after to be confident of the dissipation of any HD-tDCS aftereffect. Each condition was 
composed of three blocks with 100 trials each, always performed with the dominant hand 
(right). Each block lasted approximately 6 minutes. Between each block, one minute of rest 
was given. Overall, each participant completed 900 trials (300 trials per condition). Before 
starting the experiment, participants were trained in performing the task with 40-50 trials. 
2.5. HDtDCS protocol
The HDtDCS protocol was applied using the Starstim 8 hybrid EEG/tCS 8-channel 
neurostimulator system, designed for focal stimulation ( http://www.neuroelectrics.com). 
The protocol lasted 20 minutes at an intensity of 1.5 mA, with a ramp-up and ramp-down 
period of 1 minute each. Five stimulation PISTIM Ag/AgCl electrodes with 1cm radius were 
used to induce anodal HD-tDCS over the left IPS. According to the EEG 10-20 localization 
system, the 4x1 montage and electrode distribution was the following: The anode was placed 
over the P3 position, corresponding to the location of the left IPS following previous studies 
(Sack et al. 2002; Kim et al. 2007). The reference return electrodes were placed around at

PO7, PO3, CP1, and CP5. We configured the stimulation protocol using NIC Neuroelectrics 
software. After the stimulation, two subjects reported mild itching and stinging sensations in 
the stimulated area.
2.6. Data analysis
Temporal and spatial kinematics were inferred from the position of the cursor on the screen 
(Figure 2A). We measured reaction time (RT), movement time (MT), time to peak velocity 
(T2P) and correction time (CT) as temporal parameters for subsequent analysis in trials with 
and without target displacement. A velocity threshold of 50 mm/s in the longitudinal axis was 
used to detect the onset and the offset of the movement (Neggers and Bekkering 2002; Van 
Beers et al. 2004). RT was defined as the time elapsed between target onset and the 
movement onset. MT was obtained by subtracting the movement onset from the movement 
offset. T2P was defined as the time between the onset of the movement and the moment 
when the peak velocity was reached. CT was detected with a velocity threshold of 150 mm/s 
in its lateral component of the trajectory during the first 90% of the MT (Figure 2A, bottom 
panel). CT was normalized as a percentage of the total MT (normalized CT). Velocity was 
derived from the smoothed time series of the cursor position by first numerical 
differentiation.
(Figure 2 about here)
For each trial, we obtained two-dimensional spatial coordinates of the position of the 
hand (mouse). Two types of hand trajectories were established for displaced trials: erroneous 
trajectories and corrective trajectories. The rationale to define a hand trajectory as corrective 
or as erroneous was as follows (Figure 2B). First, we obtained the distribution of all the cursor 
endpoint positions for the undisplaced trials by calculating the ellipsoid 95% confidence 
interval (purple ellipse) (Granek et al. 2012; Messier and Kalaska 1999). Trajectories of 
displaced trials in which the cursor endpoint position falls within the abovementioned 
confidence interval were considered as erroneous trajectories (Pisella et al. 2000). 
Additionally, since the main instruction provided to participants was to hit the target as fast

and as accurate as possible, we amplified the exclusion criteria by considering also as 
erroneous all hand trajectories that ended out of the ellipsoid 95% confidence interval formed 
by the endpoints of the displaced trials in the condition prior to stimulation (green ellipses). 
Finally, trajectories that did not pass the abovementioned velocity threshold to detect CT 
were also considered as erroneous trajectories(Neggers and Bekkering 2002). The rest of 
hand trajectories were defined as corrective. All the confidence intervals were independently 
calculated for each side, condition and participant.
2.7. Statistics
Prior to the analysis, all trials with RT faster than 150ms and slower than 500ms were 
removed. Valid MT was limited to a range from 200ms to 600ms (Pisella et al. 2000). Finally, 
trials with CT below 150ms were also excluded. Four participants were excluded from the 
analysis due to poor performance (trial rejection rate higher than 30%). On the remaining 
twenty-four participants, we analyzed the behavioral aftereffects of the HD-tDCS on the 
temporal and spatial kinematic dynamics of the hand movement. It might be argued that the 
mean RT of one participant was outlier with respect to the group. However, this participant 
performed within the group range for the rest of parameters. In addition, to exclude any 
possible bias, we separately ran the statistical analyses without this participant and the results 
remained unchanged. For these reasons this participant was finally included in the group 
analysis.
We hypothesized that genuine HD-tDCS aftereffects on behavioral aspects would be 
observable by exhibiting an enhanced pattern in the condition immediately after the anodal 
HD-tDCS protocol and being at the same time comparable between the pre-HDtDCS and the 
Control conditions. We conducted linear mixed models including type of trial (displaced, 
undisplaced) and condition (pre-HDtDCS, POST-HDtDCS, Control) as fixed factors to test for 
differences in RT, MT and T2P, CT, and normalized CT. The model provided estimates for each 
factor effect. This analysis was carried out for RT without considering the ‘type of trial’ factor, 
since the target displacement always occurred after the movement onset. In the same way, 
we performed the model with condition as a single within-subject factor to test for 
differences in CT, normalized CT and the correction rate, defined as the percentage of 
erroneous trajectories. All models included participant as a random factor. Additionally, a 
regression analysis with condition order (pre-HDtDCS=1, POST-HDtDCS=2, Control=3)

assessed putative linear ‘learning’ effects. To analyze the ballistic dynamics of the movement, 
we compared the time series of both the longitudinal and lateral velocity from each condition 
by running separate t-tests for every time step. Finally, we built correlation matrices to 
evaluate the relationship between kinematics and accuracy to explore reliable indicators of a 
successful outcome when correcting the movement. 
In all analyses, the direction of the displacement (left or right) was not considered as 
a factor, since preliminary analyses had revealed a lack of statistical main effects and 
interactions (all P > 0.26). Trials with more than five consecutive missing recording samples 
were discarded from the analyses. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using paired 
sample t-tests. Threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 with Bonferroni-
correction for multiple comparisons. For all statistical effects involving two or more degrees 
of freedom, the Greenhouse-Geisser epsilon was used to correct for possible violations of the 
sphericity assumption (Jennings and Wood 1976). We report corrected p-values. All the 
statistical analyses were conducted using R 3.5.1 (The R Project for Statistical Computing; 
http://www. R-project.org/).

3.2. HDtDCS aftereffects in spatial dynamics and speed of the hand movement
Figure 5 depicts the spatial analyses of the movement trajectories and the speed profiles of 
both lateral (x) and longitudinal (y) axes. In contrast to the temporal aspects –which showed 
significant differences possibly associated to the HD-tDCS protocol– the spatial dimension of 
the movement did not show statistical differences between conditions. In general, 
participants tended to initiate the correction at comparable zones in all conditions. The speed 
profiles were decomposed in the two axes in which the movement was performed (x, y ). 
Consistently with the results of the temporal dynamics, the analysis of the bell-shaped speed 
profiles showed that the lateral deviation (x) in the pre-HDtDCS condition differed from that 
in the POST-HDtDCS and Control conditions (p < 0.01 from time = 245ms). As mentioned 
above, MT in these two conditions were also significantly faster. The difference in lateral 
velocity between POST-HDtDCS and Control was negligible. The in-depth analysis of the 
longitudinal velocity (y) revealed different strategies to perform the movement in the POST-
HDtDCS versus Control conditions. As already stated, the POST-HDtDCS movements took 
longer T2P compared to the other two conditions (all P < 0.01). In contrast, movements in the 
Control condition exhibited a more ballistic –less conservative– profile, with the highest peak 
velocity and a shorter time to reach it, likely suggesting a substantially higher acceleration.

3.3. Performance and kinematic correlates
Finally, we analyzed the relationship between temporal performance achieved by the 
participants and the kinematic aspects of the movement. Figure 6A shows the correlation 
matrix between correction rate in adjusting the movement and RT, MT, T2P, peak velocity, 
CT, and normalized CT. RT correlated negatively with success rate, suggesting that an earlier 
beginning of the movement was associated with higher chances to adjust the movement 
properly (r = -0.41, p = 0.0003). As expected, normalized CT and correction rate were highly 
and significantly correlated, reinforcing the idea that lower normalized CT values would end 
up in higher success rates. We subsequently computed the difference between the 
normalized CT and T2P in the Post- and the pre-HDtDCS conditions, as a surrogate of the HD-
tDCS aftereffect. Interestingly, we observed that the decrease in normalized CT correlated 
negatively with the increase of T2P (Figure 6B). In line with the temporal and spatial analyses, 
increased T2P might result in an extended temporal window to enhance movement 
monitoring and optimize the chances to re-orient the trajectory successfully. Lastly, such so-
called HD-tDCS aftereffect also correlated with the baseline performance in the pre-HDtDCS 
condition, in which individuals with a baseline normalized CT at ~60% of the total MT show 
from mild to no HD-tDCS aftereffect, suggesting a putative ceiling effect (Figure 6C). In other 
words, a poor baseline performance was more prone to improvement after the HD-tDCS.

4. DISCUSSION
In the present study we combined HD-tDCS and exhaustive hand tracking data to assess 
the impact of excitatory neuromodulation of IPS on online visuo-motor coordination. To that 
end, we compared hand kinematics from a double-step paradigm task before (pre-HDtDCS) 
and after (POST-HDtDCS) an excitatory HD-tDCS protocol over the left IPS, as well as in a third 
Page 12 of 27
European Journal of Neuroscience
For Peer Review
13
condition (Control) when HD-tDCS aftereffects were completely dissipated. At the 
neuromodulatory level, we established the association between increased cortical excitability 
in IPS and the enhancement of some aspects of visuo-motor control. At the behavioral level, 
we dissociated the temporal versus spatial aspects of the visuo-motor coordination, showing 
that the excitatory neuromodulation of IPS induced an enhancement of the temporal, but not 
the spatial, dynamics of the movement. 
Results showed that after 20 minutes of excitatory neuromodulation of IPS, participants 
exhibited an earlier onset of the correction of the movement (normalized CT) with respect to 
the total MT. In addition, normalized CT in the Control condition (24 hours after the POST-
HDtDCS condition) was similar to the pre-HDtDCS condition. These results point towards a 
genuine impact of the excitatory neuromodulation of IPS on the onset time of visuo-motor 
corrections with respect to the total MT. Correction rate increased after the neurostimulation 
and remained above baseline in the Control condition, suggesting a predominant role of 
learning or practice. Finally, there was a negative correlation between correction rate and 
some temporal aspects of the movement that differed in the POST- versus pre-HDtDCS 
conditions. Altogether, these results clearly indicate that increasing the cortical excitability of 
IPS affected the temporal properties of reaching movements, leaving unchanged the abilities 
to adjust such movements. 
4.1. Relationship between temporal dynamics and success rate
The analyses of movements' temporal dynamics highlighted important effects following 
the excitatory neuromodulation of IPS. Previous work showed that, in absence of neural 
stimulation, deviations of the spatial path towards a new location can happen at about 200ms 
after the target displacement (Brenner and Smeets 1997; Castiello et al. 1991). This is in line 
with our finding that the Correction Time was around 250ms in all three conditions. In 
addition, the prototypical tradeoff between speed and accuracy also establishes that faster 
movements regularly present higher error rates (Rodriguez-Herreros et al. 2015). 
Interestingly, we replicated this finding only in the pre-HDtDCS and Control conditions, and 
we further showed that the duration of correct and erroneous movements (MT) was similar 
in the POST-HDtDCS condition. These findings suggest that increasing the cortical excitability 
of IPS may facilitate the precision of rapid re-orienting of movements, which dovetails well 
with previous studies showing how the intervention of IPS function critically affects precision

and accuracy of fast movements (Pisella et al. 2000; Panico et al. 2018; Rodriguez-Herreros et 
al. 2015). Also, the increased T2P in the POST-HDtDCS condition supports our interpretation 
and opposes well to the ballistic pattern of the faster movements performed in the Control 
condition, denoting distinct kinematic strategies. Based on these results and the speed 
profiles, it is possible to predict a remarkable acceleration –2nd derivative– of the movement 
in the Control condition, compared to a particularly steady and gradual escalation of the 
movements performed immediately after the HD-tDCS protocol (Nagasaki 1989). The effect 
size is especially noticeable in the first block of the POST-HDtDCS condition, concurring with 
the peak of the HD-tDCS-induced increase in cortical excitability, and diminishing nearly 
linearly during the subsequent minutes (Nitsche and Paulus 2000). These results suggest that 
substantial changes in intraparietal excitability are required to induce observable and 
statistically reliable alterations in the movement pattern. Finally, the lack of differences in the 
spatial properties of the movement endorses the view that participants estimated a 
‘correction region’ independent of the speed or movement kinematics, relying on the time 
needed to reach this region (Rodriguez-Herreros et al. 2013).
4.2. The role of inter-individual differences 
Shedding light on inter-individual variability is critical for understanding the neural basis 
of online visuo-motor coordination. Important individual differences have been found in 
healthy individuals performing goal-directed reaching movements that required online 
adjustments towards unexpected visual perturbations (Boy et al. 2010; Reichenbach et al. 
2008). Accordingly, we also found a high degree of inter-individual differences both in the 
pre- and POST-HDtDCS conditions. Many factors can contribute to this variability (Ziemann 
and Siebner 2015). In regard to the impact of HD-tDCS, recent computational modeling 
suggests that parameters such as skull thickness, subcutaneous fat levels, cerebrospinal fluid 
density, and cortical surface topography can greatly influence current flow and density 
patterns during stimulation (Datta et al. 2012; Truong et al. 2013). In our study, we 
investigated how the inter-individual variability of the HDtDCS effects could be associated 
with specific aspects of the movement. To this aim, we correlated the decreased normalized 
CT in POST-HDtDCS with the baseline normalized CT in pre-HDtDCS. Individuals with poor 
baseline online visuo-motor coordination were more prone to enhance their performance. In 
particular, only individuals with a baseline normalized CT higher than 60% of the total MT

showed a significant decrease in the POST-HDtDCS session. Remarkably, in the POST-HDtDCS 
condition the increase in T2P correlated with the decrease in normalized CT. This association 
possibly indicates that the T2P expansion could be considered a surrogate marker of 
successful performance, helping to decipher the structural/functional correlates that 
subserve online visuo-motor control. Overall, the present study also shows that kinematic 
inter-individual differences can help to understand the inter-subject variability of the HD-tDCS 
effects.
4.3. Control for biases and possible limitations
In this study two main strategies were used to rule out possible confounding factors that 
could mislead the interpretation of the results. First, we used a wireless HD-tDCS 4x1 ring 
montage that allowed IPS to be focally targeted (Datta et al. 2009). Second, all participants 
underwent a Control condition, in which they performed the task 24 hours after the 
administration of the HD-tDCS protocol, when the effects were supposed to be completely 
vanished. This approach has been empirically proven in prior brain stimulation studies (Chen 
et al. 1997; Muellbacher et al. 2000; Siebner et al. 1999), and also accounts for the differences 
attributable to fatigue effects due to an over-exposition to the task (Rodriguez-Herreros et al. 
2015). However, some issues have to be addressed in future studies. 
First, certain HD-tDCS aftereffects compete with inherent learning effects in our 
experimental protocol. The Control condition is the last one to be performed and inevitably 
exhibits the highest component of practice and experience, whereas any presumed effect of 
the HD-tDCS on behavior is expected to be back to baseline levels 24 hours later. As a result, 
statistically significant HD-tDCS aftereffects would require an effect size large enough to 
counteract learning. This is in line with the effects size of about 50ms for RT and MT which 
we found in the comparison between the different conditions. Second, our work is indeed 
blind as to the physiological mechanisms underlying our findings. More evidence will be 
required to make strong claims about the neurobiological substrate that links the changes 
caused by the HD-tDCS and the anatomo-functional correlates of the cortical visuomotor 
circuit. Finally, while HD-tDCS has limited temporal resolution and is indiscriminate as to 
which aspects of neural processing are modulated, other variants of transcranial electrical 
stimulation can be used to target more specific aspects of neural function. One promising tool 
is transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS), which employs oscillatory electrical

stimulation to facilitate neuronal activity, albeit it can target only frequency-specific cortical 
functions(Antal and Paulus 2013; Ozen et al. 2010).
5. CONCLUSIONS
The present study shows the specific effects of excitatory neuromodulation of IPS on 
online visuo-motor coordination. While the experimental use of HD-tDCS is still in its early 
stages, its intrinsic properties in terms of stable neuroplasticity and ease of application render 
it the ideal candidate to improve conventional interventions. One of the advantages of 
HDtDCS is its passive nature; it does not require patients’ active participation nor attentional 
load. These characteristics contrast with the classic massive repetition trainings, in which 
patients are forced to adapt to exhaustive rehabilitation protocols which risk to saturate their 
physical and cognitive resources. The use of HD-tDCS by contrast, is completely task-
independent and can operate as a preparing factor to increase the baseline neural activity 
and plasticity in a very specific brain region and without the patients paying attention to it. 
On this basis, we propose that the excitatory neuromodulation of IPS through HD-tDCS 
could be considered in experimental and clinical settings as a neural warm-up to enhance 
visuo-motor training and rehabilitation.
